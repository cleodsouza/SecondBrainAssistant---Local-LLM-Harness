Note n029 (2026-09-18):
Finished Thinking in Systems (Meadows). Long note because I want to actually think about this.

The leverage points chapter is the one that stuck. Her list goes roughly from weak to strong: numbers and parameters
(subsidies, taxes, standards) are the least powerful even though they're where everyone spends their energy. Above
that come buffers, stock-and-flow structures, delays, feedback loops, information flows, rules, the power to
self-organise, goals, and at the top, the paradigm the system comes out of.

Applying it to Kestrel: for months the team's answer to drop-off was parameter tweaks - change a button colour,
shorten a label, add a progress bar. Those helped a little. The real jump came from changing the structure: cutting
five steps to three and replacing manual uploads with DigiLocker. That's a stock-and-flow change, not a parameter.
The next level up is information flow - users don't know why we need their PAN, so they hesitate. Telling them
(a short "why we ask" line) might beat any further layout change.

Applying it to running: my knee problem is a delay problem. I increased mileage, the body adapted slowly, and the
damage showed up weeks after the decision that caused it. The fix isn't "run harder", it's respecting the delay -
smaller weekly increases.

Applying it to money: SIPs are a reinforcing loop. The rule "invest first, spend what's left" is a stronger
leverage point than any individual budgeting decision.

Applying it to my career: the goal of the system I'm in rewards shipping, but promotion rewards visible impact.
If I don't change my own information flows - writing down outcomes - the work stays invisible.

Not sure what to do with all of this yet. Mostly I want to remember that the obvious knobs are usually the weak ones.
